Highlights

  • Rules deserve independence from processes and code — Ross's foundational move is separating what must hold true from how systems happen to enforce it; buried in procedural code, rules become invisible, unauditable, and unchangeable — the original argument for rules as an explicit layer.
  • Terms, facts, rules: the three-tier anatomy of knowledge — rules can only be stated against facts, and facts only against defined terms; this dependency stack means vocabulary quality caps rule quality, and both cap what any system can reliably do.
  • IT methods broke because they hard-wired the volatile part — business logic changes far faster than infrastructure, yet traditional development welds them together; separating by rate of change is the same instinct that drives vault modeling and model-driven generation.
  • Rules are for businesspeople to own — expressed in structured natural language, rules become artifacts the business can read, dispute, and change without a release cycle; governance as a first-class activity rather than an IT afterthought.
  • A rule is a lever on behavior — Ross frames rules as the direct expression of business intent: change the rule, change the operation; the shortest path from decision to effect that exists in an organization.
  • Decades early on the third pillar — written in 1998, this book already insists that knowledge work rests on data, vocabulary, and rules together; the industry spent the following decades funding the first, tolerating the second, and forgetting the third.
