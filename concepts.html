Concepts

A named vocabulary.

Model-Driven Data Engineering earns its own words. These are the coined concepts behind the method — one memorable name per idea, so a team can point at it, reuse it, and build on it. A shared language for business, engineers and AI.

Umbrella thesis

Model-Driven Data Engineering
Building scalable, metadata-driven data platforms from models — not hand-written code.

The master brand: an approach where the data platform is generated from models and metadata rather than written and maintained by hand. Models are the source of truth; SQL, tests, lineage and documentation are derived from them deterministically.
Model-Driven Foundations
The foundational principles the whole MDDE approach rests on — model as source of truth, derive rather than hand-write.

Beneath every MDDE pattern sit a few load-bearing principles. First: the model, not the code, is the source of truth — if code and model disagree, the code is wrong. Second: derive, don't hand-write — anything that can be generated deterministically from the model should be, because generated artifacts cannot drift from their source. Third: make metadata executable, so the platform's description and its behavior are the same thing. Everything else in the concept library is these three principles applied to a specific layer. The foundations matter because they are where implementations quietly fail. Teams adopt a generator but keep hand-patching its output (violating principle one), or generate SQL but hand-write tests and docs (violating principle two) — and within months the platform is model-flavored rather than model-driven. Holding the foundations is what separates Model-Driven Data Engineering as a method from code generation as a tool. They are also where MDDE joins the wider thesis: structure beats magic. The foundations are the structure — and the master line follows from them: the magic isn't in the model, it's in the structure you give it.

Signature principle

Data Discipline
The disciplined practice — standards, conventions, rules — that makes model-driven generation reliable; discipline is the precondition for automation.

Automation amplifies whatever you feed it. Feed a generator inconsistent naming, ad-hoc modeling choices, and undocumented exceptions, and it will manufacture that inconsistency at industrial scale. Data discipline is the unglamorous precondition: consistent naming conventions, one way to model each pattern, rules written down and followed, exceptions made explicit. Not because tidiness is a virtue, but because generation only works on regular input. This inverts the usual pitch about automation. Teams hope tooling will compensate for missing discipline; in practice the dependency runs the other way — discipline is what makes the tooling possible. A template can only generate what conventions make predictable; deterministic SQL generation is only deterministic because the modeling choices upstream are constrained. Every standard you enforce is a class of code you never have to hand-write again. As a signature principle, it is the human half of structure beats magic: structure is what you build, discipline is how you keep building it the same way. And discipline in MDDE is not self-denial forever — the platform progressively takes it over, as conventions become checks and checks become generation constraints, until the disciplined way is simply the only way the platform works.

Structure Beats Magic
Output quality comes from the structure around the tools, not the tools themselves.

The first principle: results are produced by deliberate structure — models, rules, metadata, patterns — not by clever prompts or hope. The same thesis that anchors the SBM personal brand, applied to enterprise data.

Business-Friendly family

Business-Friendly Data Lineage
Data lineage made clear and actionable for decision makers.

Lineage rendered for humans who make decisions — not just a technical dependency graph, but an explanation of where data comes from and what it means.
Business-Friendly Data Mapping
Bridging technical data models and the people who need to use them.

Modern platforms are full of complex metadata, lineage and technical detail. Business leaders need clarity, not code — this is the practice of delivering it.
Business-Friendly Data Modeling
Making data models clear and valuable for the business.

Models designed and presented so the business can see its own concepts in them and derive value, rather than treating the model as a purely technical artefact.
Business-Friendly Diagramming
Clear, consistent, metadata-driven diagrams that connect business and technology.

The art and practice of generating diagrams from metadata so they are always correct, consistent and readable by both business and technical audiences.
Business-Friendly Mapping
Data mappings written so business people can read, review and trust them.

Making the transformation logic between source and target legible to the business — mappings expressed as reviewable, metadata-driven statements of intent instead of opaque code.
Business-Friendly Metadata
Metadata explained and governed so it makes sense to both business and technical audiences.

Bridging data engineering and business understanding — metadata, lineage, data contracts, mappings and models explained, governed and applied so both sides understand them.
Collaborative Data Modeling
Business and engineers model together in real time — the model as a shared conversation.

Modeling as a shared, live activity rather than a handover: business experts and engineers build the model together, using business-friendly notation and diagrams generated from metadata, so the model is a conversation both sides own — not a technical artefact thrown over the wall.
Conceptual Constellations
Related business concepts mapped together as a connected constellation, not a list of isolated entities.

Business meaning does not come in isolated entities; it comes in clusters. "Order" only makes sense together with Customer, Product, Delivery, and Invoice — a small constellation of concepts whose relationships carry as much meaning as the concepts themselves. Conceptual constellations is the practice of modeling and presenting these clusters as connected wholes: pick a center of gravity, map the concepts that orbit it, and make the lines between them explicit. The constellation framing does two jobs. For the business, it is a communication device — a one-page star map of "everything around Order" is far easier to validate in a workshop than an entity list or a full ER diagram, which is where business-friendly diagramming meets semantic modeling. For the platform, it is a scoping device: a constellation is a natural unit of delivery, and its edges are candidate integration points with neighboring constellations. Stitched together, the constellations become the map of the enterprise: overlapping clusters that share stars, converging on the enterprise ontology one recognizable neighborhood at a time — bottom-up and business-first, rather than as one intimidating top-down model.

Semantic Engineering
Engineering the semantic layer deliberately — meaning treated as a built, versioned, maintained artifact.

Meaning is usually the accidental byproduct of a platform: it accretes in column names, dashboard labels, and whatever the last consultant wrote in the wiki. Semantic engineering treats it instead as an artifact you build — with requirements, versions, tests, and maintenance. Definitions are authored, reviewed, and released; changes to meaning go through the same rigor as changes to code, because in a model-driven platform a changed definition regenerates real artifacts. The engineering framing brings engineering questions to the semantic layer: What is its interface (which concepts are published, which are internal)? What is its test suite (do generated artifacts still conform to the definitions)? What is its change process (who approves a redefinition of "active customer", and how do consumers get migrated)? Guarding against semantic drift stops being vigilance and becomes regression testing. Within the semantic family, the division of labor: semantic modeling captures the meaning, semantic design shapes it well, semantic engineering keeps it true over time — and semantic data engineering is the closest sibling, the discipline of doing data engineering itself from that semantic layer. At platform scale the output of all four is the unified semantic fabric.

Semantic Modeling
Modeling at the level of business meaning — concepts, relationships, rules — before physical structure.

Semantic modeling captures what things mean before deciding how they are stored: the business concepts, how they relate, and the rules that constrain them. A semantic model says "a Customer places Orders; an Order has exactly one billing Agreement" — no tables, no keys, no load strategy. Those come later, and in MDDE they come derived: the physical model is generated from the semantic one plus architectural patterns, not designed by hand a second time. The sequencing is the point. When physical structure comes first, meaning gets reverse-engineered out of column names and tribal memory — the platform runs on guesswork. When meaning comes first, every downstream artifact inherits it: business-friendly data modeling makes the semantic model a medium the business can actually read and challenge, and the enterprise ontology gives concepts a home beyond a single project. Within the semantic family, this concept names the activity: semantic design is the design discipline around it, and semantic engineering is the practice of building and maintaining the resulting layer as an engineered artifact.

Architecture

Anti-Corruption Layer
A boundary that keeps a clean model from being polluted by messy upstream sources.

A translation boundary (borrowed from DDD, owned here for data platforms) that isolates a clean, governed model from the quirks and corruption of upstream source systems.
Architectural Governance
Governing the architecture itself — patterns, zones, standards — through the model, so structure stays coherent at scale.

Most governance concepts govern data; architectural governance governs the structure the data lives in. Which patterns are sanctioned, what each zone may and may not contain, how layers connect, what a "source access view" or "integration object" must look like — these are architectural rules, and without active governance they erode one pragmatic exception at a time until the platform is an archaeology site of abandoned conventions. The MDDE mechanism is to make the architecture rules part of the model world rather than a slide deck. When architectural patterns are encoded as templates and constraints — the stance of model-driven data architecture — the generator physically cannot emit an artifact that violates the zone rules, and model-level checks can flag a design that puts business logic in the wrong layer before anything is built. Decisions still need human judgment; architectural decision records capture the judgment, and the encoded rules enforce its outcome. This is also how architecture escapes the ivory tower: instead of architects policing compliance by review, the standards are executable and ambient, and the architects' time goes into evolving the patterns rather than defending them.

Business-Shaped Integration Layer
An integration layer whose structure follows business domains, not source-system topology.

An integration architecture organised around business entities and domains — the shape of the business, not the accident of which source systems happen to exist.
Enterprise Context Architecture
Treat the AI-context layer as a first-class, governed asset — not an afterthought.

Enterprise Context Architecture (ECA) is the deliberate design and governance of the context an organization feeds to its AI systems — the instructions, memory, retrieval sources, metadata and signals that decide what a model thinks about. It extends the data-governance mindset one layer up: where an Enterprise Ontology governs what data means, ECA governs the context that surrounds that meaning at the moment of use. Context becomes an asset with an owner, a lifecycle, quality checks and access rules — versioned and curated like any other data product, rather than pasted ad-hoc into prompts. The premise, borrowed from "Context Is King": semantics describe, but context decides — so if AI decisions are to be trustworthy and repeatable, the context layer must itself be architected and governed. ECA is the enterprise-scale counterpart of an individual's context-engineered second brain.

Enterprise Ontology
A shared vocabulary of core business concepts that grounds the whole data strategy.

A high-level, agreed set of core concepts — Party, Product, Agreement, Event — that everyone means the same thing by. The anchor that the Business-Oriented Logical Data Model elaborates into a business-shaped integration layer capturing enterprise-wide meaning, flexible enough to historize and reuse.
Reasoning Layer
The layer where conclusions are drawn — decisions and derived facts computed on top of governed meaning and context, not hard-coded downstream.

A named place in the architecture, above the semantic and context work, where the platform stops carrying data and starts concluding from it. Integration makes sources compatible; semantics fix what the data means; context supplies what the situation is. The reasoning layer is where those combine into a decision, a classification, a score, a derived fact — computed once and governed, rather than re-derived ad hoc in every report, dashboard and prompt. It is the layer AI leans on hardest, which is exactly why it needs structure under it: a model asked to reason over raw, unharmonised, meaning-less data is guessing. Treat reasoning as a layer with a boundary — what enters is governed meaning plus context, what leaves is an explainable conclusion with lineage — and AI reasoning stops being an ungoverned black box.
Source Access Views
Business-friendly views over historized raw source data.

Logical views on top of historized raw source data that rename cryptic columns, hide technical metadata and apply business-friendly filtering, so downstream modeling works against a clean, stable, legible interface instead of raw source quirks. Their design counterpart is the Logical Source Model — a reverse-engineered, business-shaped representation of a physical source system.
The Missing System
Orgs have systems of record; almost none have the intelligence layer on top — data + AI + rules.

Every organisation runs the obvious systems — accounting, CRM, HR, operations — all systems of record: authoritative, transactional, backward-looking. The one almost nobody builds is the layer above them: a system of intelligence that reads across all the records, detects what's changing, validates its own data (flag, don't guess), and senses the world outside (competitors, market). Built from data + AI + rules; rules is the forgotten third. A layer, not another silo — its value is that it's cross-cutting. Missing systems are invisible precisely because nothing breaks; you just never surface the insight, report bad data straight-faced, and notice the competitor's move a quarter late.
The Rear-View Mirror Problem
A system of record shows what's behind you; steer by it alone and you drive forward looking backward.

The sticky, exec-friendly hook for why reporting/BI isn't intelligence. A system of record is a rear-view mirror: accurate, detailed, essential — and entirely about what's already behind you. Most organisations, for all their expensive systems, steer forward by looking backward. The intelligence layer is the rest of the dashboard: detect + validate are the windscreen (what's ahead), sensing outward adds the side mirrors (what's coming up alongside). You need all three to have a sense of your surroundings rather than a precise picture of the road you've already driven.
The Validation Loop
Generate → validate → flag, don't guess → improve — and improve the checks too.

The loop that makes an intelligence layer trustworthy: rules check the output at input, intermediate and output stages; on a mismatch the system flags rather than guesses; a human resolves the flag; and the rule itself is sharpened. Two things improve together — the data and the rules. Rules are the forgotten third of data + AI + rules; without them you've built something worse than nothing, because it's wrong with confidence.
Unified Semantic Fabric
One connected semantic layer across sources and domains, so meaning is consistent platform-wide.

Every platform accumulates local semantics: this mart's definition of "customer", that pipeline's notion of "active", a third team's version of "revenue". A unified semantic fabric is the architectural counter-move — a single connected layer of concepts, relationships, and definitions that spans sources and domains, so that meaning is resolved once and referenced everywhere. "Fabric" is deliberate: not a monolithic central model that one team owns, but a woven network where domain models connect through shared concepts — the enterprise ontology provides the shared vocabulary, and something like a metadata mesh provides the distribution model. Domains keep autonomy over their detail; the fabric guarantees that where their concepts touch, they agree. In MDDE terms the fabric is not documentation — it is input. Because integration layers, exposure views, and lineage are generated from the semantic layer, consistency is not a review outcome but a structural property: two artifacts derived from the same fabric cannot silently disagree about what a customer is. This is the architecture-scale expression of what semantic design does at model scale.

Zones vs Layers
Physical location is not modeling intent — and both coexist.

A demystification: zones are physical/operational locations (Bronze/Silver/Gold, medallion); layers express modeling intent (harmonization, curation, marts). They are orthogonal, and conflating them is why medallion-vs-Data-Vault debates go in circles. Two named layers fall out: the Harmonization Layer (make sources technically and semantically compatible) and the Curation Layer (codify business rules, reference data and complex KPIs).

SQL & generation

Atomic SQL
Each SQL file is self-contained — it carries its own metadata and runs independently.

A pattern where every SQL file is self-contained with all metadata embedded as structured comments — executable on its own, yet deployable into any metadata platform.
Concern Classification
Every CTE is labelled by the kind of work it does, so humans and AI know its role.

Each transformation step is explicitly typed by its concern — source (src_), filter (flt_), aggregate (agg_), deduplicate (dd_) — one CTE, one concern, one label. A signal AI assistants read to know what a step is for, and a rule the SQL Advisor enforces.
CTE Notebooks
Interactive, cell-by-cell SQL development with metadata round-tripping and a per-CTE advisor.

A development surface where SQL is built and run one CTE at a time while metadata flows in as you shape it — not only out at deploy. A built-in SQL Advisor does per-CTE static analysis: it flags separation-of-concerns violations, scores complexity, and warns on non-determinism.
CTE Regression Testing
Compare a migrated query CTE-by-CTE to find the exact step that changed.

Automated CTE-by-CTE comparison of query results before and after a migration — finding the root-cause difference instead of only comparing final output.
Deterministic SQL Generation
Identical, repeatable SQL from the same metadata — a hard invariant.

Generating SQL that is byte-for-byte reproducible: no non-deterministic functions, no sorting ambiguity, no random tie-breaks. A hard invariant, not a nice-to-have.
Genie Meets the Cookbook
AI assistants as sous-chefs working inside a structured recipe DSL.

The framing of AI assistants as sous-chefs: they don't improvise SQL freehand, they work inside the structured Recipe Cells DSL, reading the concern of each cell and filling in the how. The cookbook (the patterns and rules) constrains the genie, so AI help stays deterministic and reviewable rather than a fresh guess each run.
Model Close to the Data
Metadata, validation and governance live where the data lives — where the SQL runs.

The architectural principle that rules and metadata should sit next to the data and the engine, not in a separate governance system that drifts out of sync.
Recipe Cells
A declarative DSL where each step compiles to one single-concern CTE.

A recipe written as declarative cells — SOURCES, CLEANSES, FILTERS, AGGREGATES, SOURCE_MERGES — each compiling to exactly one focused CTE that does one kind of work. You declare intent per step; the engine emits clean, single-concern SQL. The notebook is the kitchen, the SQL file is the dish; a seam date marks where an old source table hands over to a new one.
Round-Trip Engineering
Metadata flows into the editor as you write — not just out at deploy.

The counter to one-way generation: as you shape SQL in a notebook or annotate it SQL-first, that work flows back into the metadata, and metadata flows forward into your editing surface. Generation and authoring stay in sync in both directions, so hand-edits are first-class and drift is prevented.
Seam Date
The pivot date where an old source table hands history over to a new one.

In a SOURCE_MERGES cell, the seam date is the precise pivot point where an old source table stops and a new one takes over, with explicit rules for the overlapping history around the seam. It makes a source migration a controlled, dated handover instead of a silent discontinuity in the data.
Self-Describing SQL
SQL that carries its own meaning — metadata embedded where the query lives.

SQL files that describe themselves: structured metadata embedded as part of the file so the query explains its own schema, intent and lineage.
SQL-First Modeling
Model in SQL — the language the platform already runs — rather than a separate modeling tool.

Treating SQL itself as the modeling surface: the model lives close to the engine that executes it, avoiding a translation gap between a modeling tool and the running platform.
The Pantry Moved
A folksy name for a source-table transition — when upstream replaces a table mid-history.

The plain-language framing of a source-table transition: the ingredients didn't change, but the pantry moved — upstream swapped one table for another partway through history. It frames data migration as a continuity problem (keep cooking the same dish from a relocated pantry), resolved by a seam date in SOURCE_MERGES.
The Soup Before the Serving
The notebook is the messy kitchen; the SQL file is the clean dish served to production.

The authoring metaphor inside Recipe Cells: exploratory work happens in the notebook — the kitchen, where things are messy and iterative (the soup) — and only the finished, clean result is written to the SQL file that production consumes (the dish, the serving). Keeps exploration and delivery deliberately separate.

Capsule Slot
One of the six conventional metadata containers inside a Data Capsule.

The six conventional slots of a capsule — schema, properties, quality, contracts, classification, lineage — each a well-known place for one kind of metadata.
Common Integration Layer
A governed semantic handshake between domains and systems.

A harmonised, governed representation of business entities and relationships that acts as the shared handshake between domains and systems.
Completeness Gate
A CI gate that requires specific metadata slots before a deployment is allowed.

A CI enforcement mechanism that stages capsule adoption: it requires named metadata slots to be present before allowing a deployment, tightening strictness phase by phase.
Data Capsule
Data shipped together with its complete metadata as one versioned, executable unit.

A single, versioned, executable unit where data ships with its full metadata — schema, properties, quality, contracts, classification and lineage — in one place.
Knowledge Representation
Capturing meaning in machine-usable structured form, so systems and AI can reason over it.

Knowledge representation is the old AI discipline MDDE quietly rests on: expressing what you know — concepts, relationships, rules, constraints — in a structured, typed form that a machine can act on, not just display. A wiki page about "customer churn" is knowledge for humans; a modeled concept with typed relationships and executable rules is knowledge for the platform. This is why the form of metadata matters so much in MDDE. Free-text descriptions cannot drive generation; structured, typed metadata can. Models, an enterprise ontology, mapping specifications, and rule definitions are all knowledge representations — and the richer and more disciplined the representation, the more the platform can derive from it deterministically and the more safely AI can reason over it via a reasoning layer. The concept also explains MDDE's relevance in the AI era: large language models are strong at language and weak at ground truth. A platform whose knowledge is represented as structured metadata gives AI something solid to reason against — the difference between an assistant that guesses at your definitions and one that reads them.

Metadata as Code
Metadata lives in version-controlled files — reviewed, tested and deployed like code.

Treating metadata as a first-class code artefact: definitions, contracts and models live in version control, get pull-request review, run through CI, and deploy like any other code — no more metadata trapped in a GUI or a wiki.
Metadata Mesh
Federated, domain-owned metadata that still interoperates through shared standards.

Data Mesh's decentralisation applied to metadata: each domain owns and publishes its own metadata as a product, while shared standards and a common model let it interoperate across the enterprise — federation without fragmentation.
Metadata Operating System
Metadata as the operational centre — governing models, lineage, contracts and execution.

A vision where metadata is not documentation on the side but the operating system of the platform: it governs models, lineage, contracts and execution.
Metadata Star
Metadata as the central star everything orbits — SQL, lineage, tests, and docs are all generated from it.

Picture the platform as a solar system. At the center sits one body with all the mass: the metadata — models, mappings, rules, contracts. Everything else orbits it and shines with borrowed light: the SQL is generated from it, the tests are derived from it, the lineage is read out of it, the documentation is rendered from it. Nothing in orbit is authored independently; every artifact's position is determined by the star. The image earns its place because it makes the failure mode visible. In most platforms there is no star — SQL, docs, lineage tooling, and test suites are separate bodies on independent trajectories, and keeping them aligned is manual gravity that teams eventually stop supplying. With a metadata star, alignment is not maintained; it is inherited. An artifact cannot drift out of sync with the metadata for the same reason a planet cannot drift out of its orbit unnoticed. Metadata Star is the mental model; the metadata operating system is the same idea as an engineered system, and executable metadata is the property that gives the star its mass. If the metadata were merely descriptive, it would be decoration at the center — it is generation that creates the gravity.

Sidecar Tables
Companion metadata tables that live beside data assets without changing them.

Companion tables in the same schema and dataset that carry lineage, quality checks and masking rules alongside the primary data — metadata that travels with the asset.
Universal Data Models
Reusable patterns for clear, scalable data models.

A catalogue of reusable modeling patterns that make data models consistent and scalable across projects and domains.
Universal Metadata Models
A reusable, universal way to structure metadata for models and mappings.

A shared, reusable schema for metadata itself — so models, mappings and platforms describe themselves in one consistent way.

Lineage & governance

Collaborative Governance
Governance as a shared practice between business and engineering, not an ivory-tower gate.

Most governance programs fail on ownership: a central committee writes policy, engineering routes around it, and the business never sees either. Collaborative governance flips the model — definitions, rules, and quality expectations are authored together, by the people who own the meaning (business) and the people who make it run (engineering), in a shared, business-friendly medium. In the MDDE frame this is only practical because the model is the meeting point. When business concepts, mappings, and rules live in business-friendly metadata rather than in code, a domain expert can review and co-own them without reading SQL — and the platform still enforces them mechanically, because everything downstream is generated. Collaboration answers who governs; siblings like embedded governance and governance-as-code answer where and in what form. The payoff is legitimacy: rules that both sides wrote are rules both sides defend. It is the working antidote to ivory-tower architecture, and the same collaborative posture that collaborative data modeling brings to model design, applied to the governance layer.

Data Breadcrumbs
Trace markers left in the data itself, so any row can tell you where it came from.

Lineage carried in-band: every row (or asset) leaves a trail of markers — source, run, transformation, timestamp — so you can walk back from any value to its origin without a separate lineage system. Lineage you can follow from the data itself.
Embedded Governance
Governance built into the platform and its generation flow, not run as a separate downstream process.

Downstream governance reviews what the platform already did; embedded governance shapes what the platform can do. The checks, standards, and controls execute inside the normal flow of work — at modeling time, at generation time, in CI, at deployment — so a non-compliant artifact is never produced in the first place, rather than produced, discovered, and remediated months later. Generation is what makes deep embedding possible. In a hand-coded platform, embedding governance means asking every engineer to remember every rule; in a model-driven one, the generator is a single choke point that all artifacts pass through. Put the controls there and compliance stops being a behavior and becomes a property of the machinery — the shift-left move applied to governance, all the way to the point of creation. Within the governance cluster, embedded governance names the placement of enforcement: inside the flow. Governance DNA supplies what is enforced (traits encoded in the model), embedded metadata governance supplies the carrier (rules riding in the metadata), and governance-as-code supplies the engineering form. This concept is where they all become operational.

Embedded Metadata Governance
Governance rules embedded in the metadata layer itself, so they travel with the data wherever it goes.

Governance usually lives somewhere else — a policy portal here, a catalog there, an access-control system in a third place — and every hop the data makes is a chance for its rules to get left behind. Embedded metadata governance keeps the rules in the metadata that describes the data: classification, ownership, permitted use, and quality expectations are attributes of the dataset's metadata record, carried along whenever the metadata flows to a new tool, team, or platform zone. The design consequence is portability. Because MDDE metadata already travels — it feeds generation, catalogs, lineage tooling, and consuming domains, including across a metadata mesh — embedding governance in it means every consumer receives the rules for free, in the same payload as the structure. There is no separate synchronization problem between "what the data is" and "what you may do with it"; they are one artifact, versioned together under the metadata-as-code discipline. Within the governance cluster, this concept names the carrier: the metadata layer as the vehicle governance rides in. Its sibling embedded governance is about the flow — enforcement wired into the platform's generation and runtime processes. Carrier and flow together make governance ambient rather than administered.

Governance as Code
The whole governance layer — policies, checks, contracts — expressed and enforced as versioned, generated code.

Governance-as-code is the umbrella practice: not one policy in a script, but the entire governance layer — policies, quality checks, data contracts, access rules, standards — living as code and metadata under version control, with the same lifecycle as everything else in the platform. Governance stops being a parallel organization with its own documents and cadence, and becomes a first-class part of the engineering system: reviewed in pull requests, tested in CI, released with the platform. The MDDE twist is that much of this layer does not even need to be written — it is generated. Quality checks derive from model-level rules, contracts derive from interface definitions, lineage-based access checks derive from the mappings. The governance layer inherits the central MDDE property: derived artifacts cannot drift from their source, so the governance you can read is the governance that actually runs. Within the cluster, this concept names the form and scope of the layer. Policy-as-code is its atomic unit (one policy, one versioned rule); governance DNA is the inheritance mechanism that lets generated artifacts carry governance traits; embedded governance describes where enforcement executes. Together they replace the audit-season model of governance with a continuous, mechanical one.

Governance DNA
Governance encoded into the model itself, so every generated artifact inherits it — in the DNA, not bolted on.

DNA is the right metaphor because of inheritance. When ownership, classification, retention, and quality expectations are properties of the model — attached to the concept, the attribute, the mapping — every artifact generated from that model carries them automatically. Tag an attribute as personal data once, and every generated table, view, test, and documentation page downstream expresses that classification, because they were all synthesized from the same genome. Contrast this with bolt-on governance: a catalog team annotating artifacts after they exist, forever chasing a platform that changes faster than they can tag it. In the DNA model there is nothing to chase — an artifact cannot exist without its governance traits, because generation is the only way artifacts come into being. Coverage is 100% by construction, not by campaign. Within the governance cluster, Governance DNA names where the encoding lives: in the model, upstream of everything. Embedded governance describes the enforcement happening inside the platform flow, and governance-as-code describes the engineering form of the layer as a whole — DNA is the inheritance mechanism underneath both.

Horizontal vs Vertical Lineage
Left-to-right flow vs top-to-bottom drill-down.

Two axes of lineage: horizontal traces data flow from sources to targets; vertical traces drill-down within a single asset, from column to table.
Innocent SQL
Well-meaning queries that quietly create multiple sources of truth.

A perfectly reasonable-looking query, written in good faith, that redefines a metric or entity slightly differently from everyone else's — silently spawning a competing version of the truth. No malice, no obvious bug; just drift nobody notices until the numbers disagree in a meeting. The villain that governed mappings and deterministic generation exist to prevent.
Lineage Explorer
An interactive map of concepts, mappings, assets and releases.

An explorable graph that connects concepts, mappings, data assets and releases into one interactive map of how data flows and depends.
Living Documentation
Documentation derived from metadata and lineage — so it never goes stale.

Documentation generated directly from the operational model, metadata and lineage — it evolves with the platform instead of rotting in a wiki.
Metric Governance
Governing metric definitions so a metric means the same thing everywhere — one definition, generated and enforced.

Nothing erodes trust in a data platform faster than two dashboards disagreeing about revenue. Metrics are where semantic inconsistency becomes visible to executives, because a metric is a definition compressed into a single number — filters, grain, time window, currency handling, exclusions — and every BI tool, notebook, and export is an opportunity to recompute it slightly differently. Metric governance applies the MDDE pattern to this specific, high-stakes domain: the metric's definition lives once, as structured metadata — its formula, grain, filters, and ownership — and every place the metric appears is generated or validated from that definition. Consumption surfaces such as exposure views carry the computed metric so downstream tools inherit rather than reimplement it, and checks flag any artifact that computes a governed metric its own way. Defined in business-friendly metadata, the definition is also something a controller can read and sign off on — which matters, because a metric definition is ultimately a business decision. The result is that "which number is right?" becomes a resolvable question with an owner, a version history, and a single answer — semantic drift contained at the point where it costs the most credibility.

Automating data-quality checks with models, metadata and patterns.

Data-quality checks generated and driven by the model and its metadata rather than hand-written per table.
Policy as Code
Governance policies expressed as code or metadata — versioned, testable, and enforced automatically.

A policy in a PDF is a wish; a policy as code is a fact about the platform. Policy-as-code takes individual governance policies — "personal data never lands unmasked in the exposure layer", "every table has an owner", "metric definitions require review" — and expresses each one as an executable, versioned rule that the platform evaluates automatically, in CI or at generation time. The unit of the concept is the single policy, and that is what gives it its engineering properties. Each policy gets a lifecycle: it is proposed in a pull request, reviewed by the people accountable for it, tested against known-good and known-bad cases, versioned so you can answer "what did the policy say in March?", and enforced by machinery rather than memos. Exceptions become explicit, reviewable code paths instead of quiet Slack agreements. Closely related to governance-as-code — the sibling that names the whole governance layer built this way; policy-as-code is its atom. And in MDDE the natural home for these rules is the metadata layer itself, following the same versioned-artifact discipline as metadata-as-code: a policy over models and metadata can be checked before anything is generated, which is where enforcement is cheapest.

Platform Thinking
Treat the data platform as a product with reusable capabilities, not a pile of one-off pipelines.

Pipeline thinking asks "how do we deliver this dataset?"; platform thinking asks "what capability, once built, delivers every dataset of this kind?" It treats the data platform as a product with users (the delivery teams), an interface (models and metadata), and a roadmap of reusable capabilities — loading patterns, history handling, quality checks, exposure — instead of a growing pile of bespoke pipelines that each solve the same problems slightly differently. MDDE is platform thinking taken to its conclusion: if the capability is truly reusable, it can be generated, and the per-dataset work collapses into modeling. The metadata operating system is the platform's kernel; patterns like batteries included describe its standard library. Teams stop paying the one-off tax on every delivery because the platform, not the pipeline, carries the engineering. The economics are the argument. One-off pipelines scale cost linearly with datasets; a platform amortizes: the tenth source costs a fraction of the first, and improvements to a capability upgrade every pipeline that uses it. That is also why platform thinking is a delivery-method concern and not just an architecture concern — it changes what a team commits to per sprint, which is where model-driven agile delivery picks up the thread.

AI & innovation

Autonomous Data Engineering
Pipelines that build and adapt themselves from models plus AI — the horizon MDDE points at.

Autonomous data engineering is the end-state hypothesis: a platform where a model change is the change. New source, new attribute, changed business rule — the platform regenerates its own pipelines, tests, and documentation, and AI handles the judgment calls that templates cannot, all within the guardrails the model defines. Human engineers move from writing pipelines to curating models and rules. The critical distinction from AI hype is the order of operations. Autonomy without structure is vibe-driven AI — plausible output with no contract behind it. MDDE's route to autonomy runs through determinism first: deterministic SQL generation handles everything that can be derived mechanically, and AI is admitted only where the metadata gives it verifiable boundaries. The model is what makes autonomy safe rather than merely impressive. That makes this concept less a technology bet than a maturity ladder: hand-written → generated → self-adapting. Each rung requires richer generative models and a stronger metadata operating system underneath. The horizon is autonomous; the road there is structural.

Generative Models
Models rich enough that the platform's artifacts are generated from them — the generative core of MDDE.

Not every model can drive a platform. A boxes-and-lines diagram communicates; a generative model produces. The difference is completeness and precision: a generative model carries enough typed detail — entities, attributes, relationships, mappings, history semantics, quality rules — that SQL, tests, lineage, and documentation can be derived from it mechanically, with no human filling in the gaps between model and code. This reframes modeling effort. In documentation-driven shops, model detail is overhead — it will be out of date next quarter anyway. In MDDE, every increment of model richness is capability: add history semantics to the model and deterministic SQL generation can produce the temporal logic; add quality rules and the tests come out with the pipeline. The model's richness sets the ceiling on what the platform can generate — which is the master line in one sentence: the magic isn't in the model, it's in the structure you give it. The concept sits in the AI & innovation category deliberately (and despite sharing a name with generative AI, means almost the opposite — deterministic derivation, not probabilistic output). Generative models are also what make AI safe to add later: a model rich enough to generate from is rich enough to verify against, which is the stepping stone toward autonomous data engineering.

Continuous Defragmentation
Continuously consolidating scattered, duplicated data logic back into the model, so the platform doesn't fragment over time.

Platforms fragment the way disks do: not through any single bad decision, but through thousands of small writes in convenient places. A business rule copy-pasted into a second pipeline, a filter redefined in a dashboard, a lookup table cloned into another schema — each fragment works locally, and collectively they scatter the platform's logic across places no one can enumerate. Fragmentation is the default direction of entropy; defragmentation has to be a deliberate, continuous practice. The practice is a recurring sweep with a fixed destination: find logic that exists in more than one place — or in the wrong place — and consolidate it into the model, where it becomes one definition that generation propagates everywhere it is needed. The model is what makes defragmentation pay off; consolidating into yet another shared script just moves the fragment. Consolidating into metadata retires it: the duplicates become generated instances of a single source of truth. "Continuous" is the operative word. Done as a one-off cleanup project, defragmentation loses to entropy within a year; done as routine — a candidate list fed by lineage analysis and duplicate detection, worked down every sprint — it holds the line. It is the structural companion to watching for semantic drift: drift is meaning fragmenting, and this is the maintenance loop that pulls the pieces back together.

Continuous Metadata Integration
CI applied to metadata — metadata changes flow through the platform continuously, validated and propagated like code.

Continuous Metadata Integration (CMI) does for metadata what CI did for code: every change to a model, mapping, or rule enters a pipeline that validates it, checks its impact, regenerates the affected artifacts, and propagates the result — automatically, on every change, not in quarterly release ceremonies. A modeler merges a changed mapping; minutes later the platform has consistent SQL, tests, lineage, and documentation reflecting it, or a failed check explaining why not. The premise is metadata-as-code: once metadata is versioned in a repository, it can ride the same machinery as software — branches, pull requests, automated checks, promotion through environments. But CMI adds the metadata-specific stages that code CI does not have: semantic validation (is the model internally consistent?), impact analysis via lineage (which downstream artifacts does this change touch?), and regeneration as the deployment step, with the validation loop as the quality gate on what generation produced. CMI is what makes a model-driven platform feel alive rather than ceremonial. Without it, the model is technically the source of truth but practically a bottleneck — changes queue up for the next big regeneration, and people start patching downstream to avoid the wait, undoing the model-driven contract one hotfix at a time. With it, the shortest path to any change runs through the model, which is exactly where you want the habit to form.

Contract-Driven Data Engineering
Data contracts are the source of truth — the platform is built to satisfy them.

An approach where explicit data contracts (schema, semantics, quality, SLAs) come first and the pipeline is generated and validated to satisfy them — producers and consumers agree on the contract, and the platform enforces it. A sibling paradigm to Model-Driven Data Engineering.
Executable Metadata

Traditional metadata is a rear-view mirror: harvested after the fact, describing what the platform already does, drifting the moment anyone changes a pipeline. Executable metadata inverts the relationship — the metadata comes first, and the platform's behavior is executed from it. A mapping definition is not documentation of a load; it is the input that deterministic SQL generation turns into the load. Change the metadata, and the platform changes. Being executable is what makes metadata trustworthy. Descriptive metadata can lie because nothing breaks when it is wrong; executable metadata cannot drift from reality because it is the source of that reality. This is the mechanical heart of the MDDE thesis: documentation, lineage, tests, and SQL all stay consistent because they are all executions of the same metadata. The concept names the property; its siblings name the practices around it. Metadata-as-code gives executable metadata an engineering lifecycle (versioning, review, CI), and self-describing SQL closes the loop from the artifact side, keeping generated code traceable back to the metadata that produced it.

Feedback Loop
Engineering feedback loops — tests, validation, lineage — that make every cycle better-informed than the last.

A model-driven platform is not right on day one; it converges on right. The feedback loop is the mechanism: generate, run, observe (test results, validation failures, lineage anomalies, profiling surprises), and feed what you learned back into the model — so the next generation cycle starts from a better model, not from patched code. The direction of the feedback is the MDDE-specific part. In conventional engineering, findings get fixed in the pipeline and the design document quietly rots. Here, findings must flow back to the source of truth — the model and its metadata — because that is the only place a fix propagates from. A quality rule discovered in production becomes a model-level rule; a mapping error becomes a mapping correction; the fix regenerates everywhere it applies. Round-trip engineering is the discipline that keeps that channel open. The validation loop is the sharpest instance of this pattern — the tight generate-validate-correct cycle around generated SQL. The feedback loop is the general principle behind it: a maturing platform is one whose loops are short, instrumented, and always terminate in the model.

Semantic Data Engineering
Engineer the meaning, not just the movement — pipelines that carry business semantics.

Engineering pipelines around the meaning of data, not only its movement: business semantics, definitions and relationships are first-class metadata that travel with the data and drive generation, validation and lineage. The counter to plumbing-only data engineering.
Semantic Design
Design meaning once, at the top — then reuse it everywhere downstream.

Semantic design is the discipline of defining what data means once, deliberately and top-down, so that every downstream layer — logical model, physical model, pipeline, report, AI-context — inherits that meaning instead of re-inventing it. Semantics start at the top: the definition, the relationship, the business rule are designed before the plumbing, and everything below refers back to them. The opposite is bottom-up accretion, where meaning is guessed and re-derived at every layer and drifts apart. Where Semantic Data Engineering is the runtime method (pipelines carry semantics) and Enterprise Ontology is the vocabulary (the agreed core concepts), Semantic Design is the design-time act that produces them: decide meaning once, reuse everywhere.

Semantic DevOps
CI/CD for meaning — semantics and metadata versioned, tested and deployed like software.

The DevOps discipline applied to semantics: definitions, models and contracts flow through the same version-control, review, automated-test and deploy pipeline as code, with semantic changes gated and validated. Meaning ships on a release train, not by hand. Pairs Metadata as Code with the Completeness Gate.
